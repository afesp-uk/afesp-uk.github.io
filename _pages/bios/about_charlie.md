Since September 2025, I have been a Postdoctoral Research Scientist on the project *Isolating and Understanding the Most Predictable Components and Improving Their Representation in S2S Prediction*. My academic background is in mathematics and my current research interests are in applications of mathematical analysis to problems in meteorology.



My interest in meteorology as a field of study began while studying for an MSci in Mathematics at University College London (2014-2018), where I took a fantastic course on Geophysical Fluid Dynamics (GFD) run by Ted Johnson. After completing my master's project, supervised by David Hewett (UCL), I went on to complete a PhD in Mathematics (2018-2022) at the Maxwell Institute Graduate School for Analysis and its Applications (MIGAA), supervised by Beatrice Pelloni, David Bourne and Mark Wilkinson (Heriot-Watt University). The PhD programme broke down the false dichotomy between applied and pure mathematics and gave me a new perspective on the role of mathematical analysis in science. Reflecting this, my PhD project used rigorous theory of optimal transport to analyse the semi-geostrophic equations, which model the formation of atmospheric fronts. This led to a collaboration with John Methven (Department of Meteorology, University of Reading) and ultimately to my involvement in AFESP. The current project brings together many rich areas of mathematics - optimal transport, spectral theory, calculus of variations - with the aim of improving weather forecasts on timescales of weeks to months, and sits within the wider effort to imporove subseasonal-to-seasonal (S2S) forecasts motivated by their potential uses for climate services.



Between finishing my PhD and starting at AFESP, I was a postdoctoral researcher at the Maxwell Institute for Mathematical Sciences in Edinburgh (2022-2023), I worked part-time for the Stockholm Environment Institute (2022-2023), and I did a postdoc in the group of Bernhard Schmitzer at the University of Göttingen, Germany (2024-2025).
