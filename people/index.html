Marco Burderi

AFESP PhD Student

Department of Meteorology

University of Reading

Hi! I’m a 1st-year PhD student in the AFESP-DTP at the University of Reading, working on the sources of subseasonal-to-seasonal (S2S) predictability in AI weather emulators. Such emulators now match or surpass state-of-the-art dynamical models in NWP and remain stable at long roll-out times, but it is unclear where their S2S skill comes from. My work compares predictable signals in the extratropics, with a focus on teleconnections and jet/weather regimes, between initialised dynamical predictions and AI-trained emulators, to test whether both systems capture them for the right physical reasons. I will later complement this with parallel sensitivity experiments in the IFS (or UM) and the emulators, with the longer-term aim of informing hybrid dynamical–AI forecasting approaches.

My lead supervisor is Chris O’Reilly (University of Reading, Department of Meteorology).

Before starting my PhD, I completed a BSc in Physics at Sapienza University of Rome and an MSc in Physics (Science of Climate) at the University of Bologna. My MSc thesis, carried out within the ENCIRCLE project with CNR-ISAC Bologna, used Destination Earth storyline simulations to assess the response of extreme precipitation over Italy to anthropogenic warming. I then spent eight months as a research assistant at the University of Bologna and CNR-ISAC, studying how climate change affects major Italian extreme precipitation events in high-resolution nudged simulations.

Professor Hannah Cloke OBE is Regius Professor in Meteorology and Climate Science at the University of Reading, one of a small number of Crown appointed chairs in UK science, and a world leading authority on floods, climate extremes and disaster risk reduction. She studied Geography (BSc, 1999) and Hydrology (PhD, 2003) at the University of Bristol, then held research positions at the European Commission Joint Research Centre, King's College London and Uppsala University before joining the University of Reading in 2012.

Hannah's research focuses on flood forecasting, early warning systems and translating evidence into action before disasters unfold. She works with operational, government and humanitarian partners including the Met Office, the Environment Agency, the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) and the Red Cross Red Crescent Movement. A Research Fellow at ECMWF, she has helped develop forecasting and warning approaches with impact. Methods she co-developed now sit within the Copernicus Emergency Management Service, including the Global Flood Awareness System and the European Flood Awareness System, improving the accuracy and lead time of flood warnings for millions of people worldwide. She was awarded the European Geosciences Union's Plinius Medal in 2018, and appointed OBE in 2019 for services to flood forecasting and hazard early warning systems.

Hannah is also one of the UK's leading public voices on weather, climate and flood risk, appearing regularly in broadcast and print media and engaging directly with policy makers, driven by the conviction that climate science only earns its place when it reaches people in a form that helps them act. She works with the film, radio and television industry, including shaping the storytelling around a flood on BBC Radio 4's The Archers, and is a regular public speaker, including at the 2026 Reading Climate Festival.

AFESP PhD student

Department of Meteorology

University of Reading

Hi! I am a PhD student at the University of Reading since September 2025, working on grey-zone convection parametrisation. I am supervised by Prof. Robert Plant and Prof. Chris Holloway from the University of Reading, and Dr. Ian Boutle from the Met Office. My project investigates the feasibility and possible benefits of coupling convection schemes to dynamics (and other parametrisations) across different grid resolutions - for example we may choose to run dynamics, microphysics, and turbulence at a O(1km) scale while keeping parametrised convection at the O(10km) scale outside the grey zone scale, therefore sidestepping or at least mitigating the convective grey zone problem. I primarily do idealised modelling with the CoMorph convection scheme, and the Met Office's next-generation atmospheric model LFRic - particularly because LFRic's model architecture allows for computationally efficient coupling between nested grids at different resolutions. Prior to my PhD, I did my Bachelors in Physics at the National University of Singapore (2021-2025). My honours project was completed under joint supervision with the Centre for Climate Research Singapore (CCRS) and investigated the nonlinear stochastic recharge oscillator model of ENSO as a possible diagnostic for nonlinear ENSO processes in CMIP6 simulations.

I am an AFESP Research Fellow at the University of Reading, where I lead the project Resolving Rain. In this project, I investigate the benefits of global km-scale models for forecasting impactful precipitation events, and how we can use satellite observations to quantify these benefits. By studying precipitation processes that emerge at the km-scale, I hope to provide clearer evidence on when and why explicitly resolved convection improves forecasts and where it doesn’t. The focus is on recent experiments in global km-scale forecasting, including, for example, ECMWF’s Weather-Induced Extremes Digital Twin and forecasts conducted by the UK Met Office.

I have a PhD from the University of Gothenburg and before joining the University of Reading in May 2026, I was a postdoctoral fellow in the Advanced Study Program at the National Center for Atmospheric Research in Boulder, Colorado (2023–2026). During my time at NCAR, I developed a framework to analyze precipitation efficiency in severe convection, as one aspect that influences how much precipitation we get at the surface, where it ultimately influences us. I am planning to apply this framework as part of my AFESP project to test if a better representation of precipitation efficiency contributes to better forecast of impactful precipitation events.

Dr Mark Muetzelfeldt

AFESP Fellow

Department of Meteorology

University of Reading

I’m Mark Muetzelfeldt. I’m an AFESP research fellow, working on how we can use high-resolution models to improve convection parametrization schemes in coarser models. In my project ALOFT (aligning organized features in coarse- and high-resolution simulations), I’m going to build a framework for identifying biases in parametrization schemes in atmospheric models compared to a high-resolution model “truth”.

Instead of targeting all biases, I’m going to focus on the organization of convection: mesoscale convective systems. These are better represented by 5-km models than by 20-km models, so they are an ideal phenomenon to use in this project. They are also challenging to parametrize, and this project should shed light on how to do this. Once I’ve built the framework, I’ll use the information to improve the CoMorph parametrization scheme in the coarser model so that it can better represent organized convection.

I’ve been based at the University of Reading since 2015, when I started my PhD on representing organized deep convection in atmospheric models. I like it so much that I’ve never left! Since finishing my PhD, I’ve worked on convection over China (COSMIC), parametrizing mesoscale convective systems in atmospheric models (MCS:PRIME) and understanding the links between updrafts and anvil clouds (AFESP project UPFLO).

Professor Ted Shepherd FRS

Grantham Professor of

Climate Science

Department of Meteorology

University of Reading

Ted is a specialist in large-scale atmospheric dynamics and circulation and its role in climate variability and change, including extreme events. He was originally trained in Math & Physics and Geophysical Fluid Dynamics, and spent 24 years on faculty at the University of Toronto before moving to Reading in 2012. Ted has held leadership roles in international scientific assessments of climate (IPCC) and stratospheric ozone (WMO/UNEP), and in the World Climate Research Programme (WCRP); was Chief Editor of the Journal of the Atmospheric Sciences (the premiere journal in fundamental aspects of atmospheric science) from 2001-2005; co-authored the US National Academy of Sciences report on Extreme Weather Events and Climate Change Attribution (2016); and chaired the Science Review Group of the Met Office Hadley Centre (2017-2021). He was elected Fellow of the Royal Society in 2016. Ted’s recent research has pioneered a ‘storyline’ approach to representing the deep uncertainty in aspects of climate variability and change related to atmospheric circulation, including extreme events. This has motivated him to question much of the current statistical practice within climate science and to begin engaging with stakeholders (e.g. on drought risk), and in inter-disciplinary collaborations (with philosophers of science, psychologists, and an anthropologist). He currently co-chairs the WCRP’s Lighthouse Activity ‘My Climate Risk’.

Ted’s interest in the AFESP programme lies mainly in the S2S prediction theme, where model deficiencies, limited observational constraints and statistical non-stationarity challenge traditional statistical approaches and call for novel, physically informed and interpretable representations of uncertainty and risk.

I’m a PhD student that has been working in the Department of Geography and Environmental Science under the supervision of Shovonlal Roy (University of Reading) and David Ford (Met Office) since September 2025. My research focuses on how data assimilation is used to improve forecasts and reanalysis datasets of ocean biogeochemistry, which includes variables such as plankton and nutrient concentrations. I work with the Met Office’s Forecast Ocean Assimilation Model (FOAM), coupling the MEDUSA biogeochemical model to NEMO ocean physics and using NEMOVAR to assimilate satellite observations of phytoplankton chlorophyll and carbon. By improving the quality of these forecasts and reanalyses, scientists and governments are better able to monitor the past and current state of the health of our oceans, identify how best to take action to prevent ecological and economic damage and meet environmental reporting obligations.

Some of the topics I plan on researching include:

  • Investigating whether satellite observations of both phytoplankton chlorophyll and carbon can effectively constrain forecasts of nutrient concentration.
  • Using ensemble data assimilation to estimate how biogeochemical parameter values vary seasonally and across different regions of the ocean.
  • Differentiating between harmful and non-harmful species of phytoplankton, and seeing whether data assimilation can improve forecasts of the concentration of toxins that harmful species produce.

Before I started my PhD, I studied for an MSci in Mathematics (Climate Science) at the University of Exeter. My dissertation studied the development and characteristics of vegetation patterns in semi-arid regions. From June-September 2023, I worked as a research intern at Mount Royal University in Calgary, AB, Canada through the Mitacs Globalink research internship scheme, studying data assimilation applied to epidemiological models. Through these experiences, I have developed an interest in working at the interface of applied mathematics, meteorology and ecology, which drew me to apply to the AFESP DTP.

In addition to receiving funding from the University of Reading through the AFESP DTP, I am also sponsored by the Met Office through the CASE studentship scheme.
